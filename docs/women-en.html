Investigation by: Eman Mounir

Seventeen years ago, Nabila packed up her life and moved to Sayyidna al-Khidr, a village tucked away in the Yusuf al-Siddiq district of Fayoum. It is a long haul from Cairo—more than four hours bumping along roads that cut through the Western Desert. When she finally got there, she found nothing but dry emptiness. No shops, no hospital, not even a bakery in sight.

All this time, Nabila had kept searching for the so-called "green paradise" everyone talked about. But under her feet, there was nothing but salty, stubborn land that wore her down year after year. She had left Beni Suef behind, hoping to give her kids a better life with land she could finally call her own. She sold her house, parted with ten carats of her old fields—everything she had—just to buy one and a half feddans in this new reclamation project people called "Graduates' Lands." They swore it was the opportunity of a lifetime.

She envisioned a future where she could cultivate wheat, clover, and corn to provide for her family and livestock, finally free from working other people’s fields. But then she faced the saline water. No wheat. No clover. No vegetables. Just olive trees, hanging on through each dry season, barely enough to pay the cooperative what she owes for the land.

So now, Nabila owns land in name only. She spends her days working on someone else’s farm. She is out the door before 7:00 AM, standing in the heat until 3:00 PM, earning 200 Egyptian pounds—that’s about four dollars—per day. When she gets home, she still has more domestic tasks waiting for her. Even after open-heart surgery, her body refuses to give her a break. Nothing about her health changes the bitter truth for women in her village: either work all day under the burning sun, or go hungry.

Nabila’s story is not unusual. She is just one of many women in the reclaimed villages near Yusuf al-Siddiq. Their families got land through the “Graduates” project, but in the end, these women are the ones left holding everything together. They work the fields. They pay off the land. They feed their families. All the while, saline water keeps eating away at the earth beneath their feet.

In the fourth part of the “Green Paradise” project, we are going back to where it all started. The big question now—how did the “Graduates’ Lands” project go from a promise to a daunting burden on the backs of women, who were left to deal with ruined fields, barely any water, and crops that wither away every season, leaving them to fight the desert on their own?

Graduate Initiatives

Mubarak's National Project for Young Graduates, initiated in 1987, was implemented under former President Hosni Mubarak (1982–2011) and former Minister of Agriculture Youssef Wali (1982–2004) as a component of the expanded land reclamation initiatives. This project aligned with the government's narrative on the importance of "redistributing the population," "addressing the unemployment crisis," and "achieving food security" through the development of agricultural land in desert regions.

In this framework, the state presented the Graduates Project as a socially responsible initiative aimed at supporting disadvantaged populations and facilitating youth employment. From 1987 to 2003, a total of 69,962 land parcels were allocated to approximately 75,000 families, including impoverished farmers, recent graduates, and agricultural laborers. Beneficiaries were granted long-term usufruct rights, typically for a period of thirty years prior to transfer of ownership, to small parcels of desert land ranging from 2.5 to 10.4 feddans. The land allocation process proceeded through a public lottery system within each governorate, following the submission of applications to local committees. Successful applicants were provided with a rural residence and an adjoining plot of agricultural land.

Mubarak's Graduates Project, initially intended to benefit young people, was later broadened to include female heads of households as a means of supporting women and tenants adversely affected by Rent Law No. 96 of 1992. This law, enacted in the mid-1990s and commonly known as the "Eviction Law" by farmers, significantly altered the circumstances of hundreds of thousands of agricultural workers. It abolished the existing agricultural tenancy system, which had offered tenants a degree of security, and facilitated the full deregulation of rental rates.

Between 1992 and 1997, farmers hit a wall. Suddenly, landlords demanded sky-high rents—sometimes four times what they paid before. If farmers couldn’t afford it, they had to walk away from the land they had worked for years. A significant surge of evictions swept across the countryside when the grace period under Law 96 ran out. Thousands of families lost everything they depended on. Some researchers say about a million farmers got caught in this mess.

Barren Land

In response to widespread anger over Law 96, the government sought to calm things down by adding people adversely affected by Law 96 to Mubarak’s Graduates’ Initiative. They handed out similar plots in desert reclamation areas. Still, a lot of beneficiaries—like Nabila, Suaad, and Reem—say these new lands just can’t replace the rich soil they lost in the Nile Valley.

Suaad says that her family got this land because her father-in-law was one of those adversely affected by Law 96. “We inherited the land from him and relocated to Fayoum,” she says, “but when we arrived, it was just empty desert. We tried planting, we worked hard, but the water is salty and the soil does not give anything back. The crops keep dying. The land is barren—it won’t grow a thing.”

Suaad does not sugarcoat it—farming out here barely exists, not even for crops that are supposed to handle salty soil. She says, “Even olives, which everyone grows, only give us fruit one year, then nothing for the next three. And when it comes to wheat, we are happy if we get just three measures. That is considered a win.”

She can’t help but compare this arid terrain to where she grew up, back in Beni Suef in the Nile Valley. “The rich clay soil in our village was generous. One feddan could give you thirty or forty thousand... of wheat. Here, it is a whole different story. We get almost nothing, and if we manage a little, we actually celebrate,” she said.

She says that the elevated salinity levels have pushed women to take up farm work just to make up for the land losses and make ends meet: “We have to leave home and do whatever we can—carry fertilizer, harvest fodder, anything that pays for the day. After all that, we go back and try to plant the land, but honestly, it gives us nothing.”

She goes on, “At first, everyone was excited. We all thought this land would be just like the fields back in our villages—fertile, productive. But here?! There is nothing. They say they gave us the land for free, but what does ‘free’ even mean? They find a way to get paid in the end.”

Nabila is in the same boat. She says, “No one told us the land was bad. We thought it would be like what we had before—beans, vegetables, enough to live on. But we found nothing but salt; hardly anything grows except a few olives, and even those take so much work. Instead of finding comfort in our land, we ended up looking for work outside, just to get by each day.”

The Issue of Salinity

The report's author cites researcher interviews confirming that agricultural expansion in Fayoum is significantly constrained by its location in a depression, leading to elevated soil salinity levels.

Dr. Ahmed Younis, a professor of physical geography at Fayoum University, puts it this way: “Yusuf al-Siddiq sits at the far end of the irrigation canals, so it gets the smallest share of water—and it’s the saltiest, too. When farmers pour on more water than the land actually needs, that extra water turns into highly saline wastewater. It pulls salts from the soil, and then people reuse that same water for irrigation. Each time, the salinity levels go up, trapping the area in a cycle that just keeps getting worse.”

A 2019 study, "Spatial Distribution of Calcium Carbonate, Salinity, pH, Soil Texture, and Organic Matter in the Soil of Yusuf al-Siddiq Center, Fayoum Governorate," revealed that the lands within the Yusuf al-Siddiq Center in Fayoum Governorate exhibit significantly elevated and widespread salinity levels, surpassing those of other centers in the Fayoum region. The study concluded that salinity is a prominent characteristic of both the surface and subsurface soil layers in the area.

The surface layer (0–10 cm) exhibited salinity values ranging from 0.68 to 132 dS/m, with a mean of 10.81 dS/m. A substantial 91.23% of the area presented salinity levels exceeding 4 dS/m, a recognized scientific threshold indicative of soil degradation attributable to salinity. Furthermore, 47.62% of the surface soil demonstrated salinity levels surpassing 10 dS/m, a concentration known to impede the growth of most conventional agricultural crops.

the subsurface layer (10–50 cm), salinity ranged from 0.92 to 82 dS/m, averaging 7.33 dS/m. Salinity exceeded 4 dS/m in 78.97% of this layer, highlighting that the salinization issue extends beyond the surface level and permeates the soil profile. Additionally, 30.39% of the subsurface layer exhibited salinity levels exceeding 10 dS/m, underscoring the widespread nature of the salinization problem.

These elevated salinity levels are attributed by researchers to a combination of factors, prominently including the prevailing hot and arid climate, which intensifies evaporation rates and facilitates the upward migration of salts. Contributing factors also encompass a high water table, insufficient drainage infrastructure, and the presence of heavy clay soils that restrict water permeability.

The elevated carbonate content of the parent soil contributes partially to the problem. The study backs up the absence of a direct correlation between the salinity levels of the Yusuf al-Siddiq soil and those of Lake Qarun. It indicates that salinization originates from inherent soil properties and water management techniques employed in the center.

The study reveals that elevated salinity presents a substantial impediment to agricultural land reclamation, resulting in diminished productivity, compromised soil integrity, and an augmented demand for freshwater resources to facilitate salt leaching. The study’s recommendations include enhancing drainage systems, cultivating salt-tolerant crops, incorporating soil amendments, and implementing optimized water management protocols.

The study findings reveal that salinity affects over 90% of the surface layer in the Yusuf al-Siddiq center, with salinization extending to subsurface depths in approximately 79% of the sector. This widespread degradation requires urgent and effective interventions in irrigation practices, drainage infrastructure, and land-utilization management.

Nader Saleh, Professor of Physical Geography at Fayoum University, puts it plainly: "The Fayoum Depression's unique characteristics pose significant structural obstacles to any agricultural reclamation initiatives in the area. Specifically, agricultural drainage ends up pooling in Lakes Qarun and Rayyan, leading to a progressive increase in water and soil salinity within the Yusuf al-Siddiq villages.”

He goes on to say that villages situated at the terminal points of canals, including Sayyidna Musa and al-Khidr—home to women such as Nabila, Suaad, and Reem—reportedly do not receive their designated freshwater allocation. Consequently, residents are compelled to utilize saline drainage water for land irrigation. It is no surprise that farming just “does not work out” in those reclaimed areas.

He points out that the compulsory transition to growing olives and sugar beets, owing to their salinity tolerance, has resulted in a notable reduction of job opportunities for women in agriculture. He also mentions that the absence of essential infrastructure, including electricity, potable water, and various services, has further hindered the Young Graduates Initiative in these areas.

A study by Professor Nader Saleh, utilizing satellite imagery by Landsat 5, Landsat 7, and Landsat 8 from 1984 to 2020, demonstrates a substantial transformation in the land cover of the Fayoum Governorate. Over almost forty years, the analysis indicates a considerable reduction in agricultural land concurrent with a notable expansion of urban areas, which have emerged as the dominant characteristic of the region's landscape.

Professor Nader Saleh attributes the observed transformation to several interrelated factors. These include sustained population growth, which has generated significant housing demand, alongside the fragmentation of agricultural land and the development of extensive road networks. Furthermore, inadequate irrigation infrastructure has led to water scarcity in areas located at the distal ends of canals, stemming from both inefficient distribution practices and unauthorized water diversion near the canal headwaters.

Professor Nader Saleh notes the continuing reduction in agricultural land, stating, "This trend has negatively affected the per capita availability of productive land and staple crops. The contraction of cultivated areas has resulted in a decline in per capita grain and cereal consumption, thereby exacerbating concerns regarding food security within the governorate."

Analysis of satellite imagery from 1984, 1990, 2000, 2010, and 2020 points out a marked change in land cover composition. Over the years, green spaces and cultivated land have decreased in size, while built-up areas and urban development have continued to expand. At the same time, more patches of land turned barren and stopped producing much of anything.

The research indicates that inefficient irrigation practices are a significant contributor to this decline. Inadequate water distribution to the far ends of canal systems has resulted in elevated salinity levels and diminished land productivity, particularly in areas distant from water sources. This systemic deficiency in water distribution compromises the long-term viability of reclaimed land, rendering it susceptible to accelerated degradation and reduced agricultural sustainability.

Professor Nader Saleh highlights the socio-economic ramifications of this environmental degradation. The reduction in arable land and scarcity of irrigation water have precipitated the emigration of agricultural workers from rural areas, a decrease in families' dependence on land as a principal source of income, and many farmers end up looking for other jobs. Consequently, the consequences of land-use changes are not confined to environmental concerns; they also affect the fabric of rural communities and the long-term financial stability of families.

Female Agricultural Workers

Reem—though everyone calls her Umm Abdullah—has a story that echoes Nabila’s and Suaad’s, and so many other women in these reclaimed villages. But when her husband passed away, everything changed. Suddenly, she had to handle everything alone: four kids depending on her, a patch of land that was supposed to keep them steady, and this constant hope that things might settle down. Instead, it all weighs on her, heavy and relentless.

She is up before sunrise, working the fields until noon. Then she heads back home, juggling chores and kids, barely catching her breath. Most afternoons, you’ll find her sitting by the door, waiting for someone to call her back to the fields. Every day, she hopes the 200 Egyptian pounds she earns will stretch far enough to fill her children’s plates.

But no matter how hard she works, the land just won’t give back. Reem says, “We sow the seedlings over and over, and they just die. We have tried planting the wormwood seedling multiple times, yet it perishes every time. We have sown clover again and again, and it meets the same fate—still nothing. Even when we use manure and fertilizer, the salinity in the soil kills everything.”

Regarding the irrigation water, it poses yet another formidable challenge. Reem says, "The water is so saline, and it almost looks blue. I have to keep the soil damp, or it dries out completely, but even then, the plants fade away right before my eyes.” Every time she fails, she scrapes together whatever little money she has left and gives it another shot. “I sow clover three times, hoping to feed the animals, but it never grows.”

Now, there is not a single woman in the village who can just farm her own land. They all work on big farms outside of the village. Nabila toils every day, and Souad does too, trying to keep up the land she got from her father-in-law. Nabila says, “Women here are more worn out than the men. We carry about 80% of the work on our backs.”

Just after sunrise, the women set off for the mountain. They gather wormwood, clear the ground beneath the olive trees, and pitch in on new farms that always seem to need cheap labor. One woman sums it up simply: “We have to work with them. We pick everything—wormwood, onions, whatever needs doing—and clear the land, just so we can take care of our families.” Nabila shares her story, and it is a long, exhausting one—seventeen years and counting. “Ever since I came here, we have been hoping the land would finally give us something back so we could rest for a while, but it never has. So day after day, I go out to work, on my feet longer than even most men can handle.”

The women start at 7:00 AM and do not finish until 3:00 PM. For all that, they earn just 200 Egyptian pounds, when a family actually needs around 500 a day just to cover the basics. Still, most of the heavy lifting and strenuous tasks fall to the women. “These days, it is us—women—who gather the wormwood, haul the baskets, and clear the land, hoping the trees will finally start to bear fruit.”

Professor Younis emphasizes that women receive the hardest hit from the salinity crisis in the reclamation villages. In his words, they are “the last pillar on which agriculture in Fayoum relies.” As the land becomes less profitable and more men leave farming for other work, women end up carrying the load by themselves. Every day, they head out to the fields, trying to make up for the shortage of male labor. The work has become tougher, too—the saline soil makes everything harder. They plant and replant, add fertilizer again and again, and do whatever they can to rescue crops that often wither before they have even had a chance to grow.

He points out that when salinity levels in the soil go up, farms do not just produce less—they also need twice as much work and money to keep going. That extra workload and expense land on women, especially those earning daily wages. As output drops, jobs dry up, and wages decline, but the cost of stuff like fertilizer, chemicals, and water just keeps climbing. In families scraping by, Professor Younes explains, women—whether they are the main earners, wives, or supporting families while their husbands migrate—are always the ones who take the first hit. They have to figure out how to fill the growing gap between what the land needs and what it actually gives back.

Decades after the government’s big promises, life in the reclaimed villages looks nothing like what anyone hoped. Take Nabila, Suaad, and Reem—they all tell the same story. The state promised a new beginning, but these women ended up carrying that weight alone. The soil is saline, water barely trickles in, and the strenuous work never ends. In the land that was hailed as a “new chance for life,” women labor harder than the very soil itself. Yet they persist—not due to the land's productivity, but because they are the last line of defense against the collapse of these communities. If they leave, the villages fall apart.

Every day, they push through the heat and dust. Reem keeps hoping her kids will get the education she never had. Nabila is still getting her strength back after heart surgery. Suaad is trying to recover from cancer that she only found by accident, because there is not even a hospital in the whole village. Even so, they keep going. That is just what they do.
