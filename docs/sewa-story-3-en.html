Story: Sayed Momia

A forty-year-old land reclamation worker, Sayed Momia assists investors in developing their extensive plots on the periphery of the Siwa Oasis. Yet, he doesn’t see himself just as a laborer in contemporary farming; instead, he views himself as a native son of the oasis, nurtured among its date palms and olive groves.

He recalls a time when owning just half an acre could sustain a comfortable livelihood: "In those days, a single palm tree could yield anywhere from 100 to 200 kilograms of dates. The harvested dates were preserved and transformed into ajwa, while the olives were pressed into sufficient oil to sustain a family for a whole year. Our diet consisted mainly of dates and olives, complemented by basic vegetables such as molokhiya and okra."

But that picture has shifted. "Contemporary farming that arrived in Siwa transformed everything," states Momia. "It diminished productivity, accompanied by a noticeable alteration in the oasis's irrigation methods and water availability.”

Alongside this, the quality of well water has deteriorated. As more wells are being constructed, particularly by new investors, the water’s purity has diminished, and salinity levels have risen. He notes, "Wells that once supplied fresh water now have salt contamination, altering the taste. This harm has impacted the older agricultural lands, leading to the desiccation of whole areas, including Bahey El-Din and El-Maraqi."

Momia identifies investment expansion as a key driver of the crisis. He remarks, "A well is intended to support 50 or 100 acres, yet an investor arrives and drills a well that services merely three acres. This disrupts the established balance; the water surpasses its requirements and gets redirected into agricultural drainage." As the groundwater rises, the fields turn into marshes or are overwhelmed by salinity. He further warns: "Agricultural drainage is increasing, and if not managed adequately, the whole oasis risks being flooded."

Siwa has a long-standing reputation for its indigenous olive varieties, including Siwi, Hamur, Shamlali, and Maraqi. Sayed refers to these olives as "unmatched, and their oil is highly sought after." However, the introduction of new, high-yield varieties like Spanish, Tuffahi, and Kalamata, has transformed the region's agricultural practices. These new varieties are cultivated intensively, with trees planted at a density of one tree per three meters, in stark contrast to the traditional spacing of one tree per twenty meters. This shift leads to swift production and substantial yields, mainly aimed at export beyond Egypt.

Momia expresses concern that this focus on intensive olive farming represents a short-sighted investment strategy, saying: “While these varieties offer ease of cultivation and rapid production, they do not match the quality of the indigenous Siwi olives.” He fears that the emphasis on quick profits will lead to the decline of these local varieties, which require more time and patience to mature and produce their distinctive oil.

In spite of the grim picture he paints, Momia holds tightly to his vision. He remains committed to preserving the land, determined not to squander its potential. He expresses, "We seek sustainable investment, effective irrigation, and equitable marketing. With proper water management and adequate care for the land, Siwa can reclaim its former prosperity."
