Story: Haider Abdel Salam

At 5:00 AM, I met Haider Abdel Salam on his property. While I was still adjusting to the early hour, Haider and his young children, all under the age of ten, were already tending to the trees on the farm, pruning and maintaining them.

Haider began working the land at the age of six, collecting fallen dates to feed his livestock. Now, forty years later, he still humbly identifies himself as "a farmer from Siwa." His story, from those early days to his current responsibilities, reflects a multi-generational commitment to farming in Siwa, a legacy passed down from father to son, as he shared in our interview.

Haider's relationship with the land deepened with experience and increasing responsibilities. Following his military service, he dedicated himself entirely to his farm. Farming became his sole profession, as he hired workers and shared the labor.

Haider described the government's attempts to address the water crisis in Siwa: "Two decades ago, the state began drilling compensatory wells to a depth of 100–150 meters, and later, deep wells, employing a 1:9 freshwater-salt water mixing system to improve irrigation." Haider has observed an improvement in water quality on his land in the last three or four years, but he views this as merely the beginning of an ongoing process.

He also believes that the issue of water access is not a conflict between "residents" and "investors," but rather a matter of equitable rationing and responsible water management that applies to everyone: "We must recognize the value of water per unit and rationalize our withdrawals, regardless of whether we are Siwans or investors," Haider explained.

He recounted that his farm once sustained a diverse range of trees, including citrus fruits, oranges, guavas, pomegranates, palm trees, and olives. However, beginning in 2013–2014, he started to observe the first signs of change: the water's taste altered, and subsequently, the fruits of the land were affected. Dates shrank in size, and overall productivity declined. Gradually, the more sensitive trees perished, leaving only pomegranates, palm trees, and olives, which were also impacted by the deteriorating, increasingly saline water quality.

Haider attributes this shift in water quality to three interrelated factors: the increase in arable land; the influx of investors utilizing deep drilling and aggressive pumping techniques; the pressure on ancient Roman wells in the farming region; and the agricultural drainage dilemma, which has led to the accumulation of saline surface water in lakes, resulting in a rise in groundwater levels and surface land salinity. "Without proper drainage treatment, Siwa has no future," remarks Haider.

However, Haider acknowledged that the deteriorating water quality on his established farm has compelled him, like many others, to seek out and reclaim new land further from the original oasis, where citrus fruits, fruits, vegetables, and other crops can be cultivated again. Meanwhile, interest in the older farms has waned, with farmers focusing primarily on seasonal harvests. "We didn't want to, but the water changed, so we had to move further away so we could feed our children,” he concluded somberly.
