Abu Bakr Baghi inherited 18 acres in Siwa, a family property for over a century, renowned for producing premium dates and olives. Approximately 50 years ago, however, Baghi observed the encroachment of a lake, formed by agricultural drainage from newly reclaimed land, onto his historic acreage. This resulted in the loss of approximately 10 acres and left the remaining eight severely affected by salinity, which decimated crop yields.

Baghi, with the assistance of his sons, Omar and Ibrahim, endeavored to construct a small tourist hotel on a portion of the salinized land to offset financial losses. Regrettably, water infiltration and salt accumulation rendered the rooms uninhabitable, and guests soon stopped coming.

“We are perpetually combating the lake and agricultural drainage,” states Omar, the eldest son. “Annually, with the onset of winter or rising water levels, we are compelled to invest significantly in importing sand to erect barriers between the water and the land. Despite these efforts, success is not guaranteed. Escalating water levels can overwhelm our defenses, particularly during severe storms. Our accumulated investments and cultivated vegetation are often instantaneously destroyed by the combined impact of agricultural drainage and the encroaching lake, necessitating costly reconstruction.”

The Baghi family's property overlooks Lake Siwa, locally known as "Al-Maraqi," one of four lakes resulting from the accumulation of agricultural drainage water in the oasis since the 1970s. This accumulation stems from the expansion of agricultural reclamation projects on the oasis's periphery, undertaken by both individual investors and small-scale enterprises owning limited land areas, as well as large corporations that have reclaimed over 30,000 acres.

This expansion occurs in the absence of a drainage system external to the Siwa Depression, which functions as a natural basin. Consequently, saline drainage water accumulates within the ancient city of Siwa, situated in the depression's center, impacting the lands of the Baghi family and their Berber neighbors, the oasis's indigenous inhabitants.

Geographic Information System (GIS) data and satellite imagery analysis reveal that the area of Lake Siwa alone increased by 21% between 1975 and 2025, while the combined area of the drainage lakes expanded by 35.12% in just five years.

In the second phase of the project Green Paradise, utilizing geographic information systems (GIS) techniques and testimonies from oasis residents, we document the ecological changes that the historic oasis has experienced over the last fifty years (1975–2025). This includes the inundation and salinization of sections of the land traditionally held by the indigenous community, compelling them to leave in pursuit of alternative sources of income. Furthermore, we highlight how the rising number of wells drilled to support agricultural reclamation initiatives and export-oriented crops has intensified the agricultural drainage crisis.

Due to the oasis's distinctive geographical setting and ongoing water drainage issues, government initiatives have further intensified the crisis. As part of the 1.5 Million Feddan Project, the New Egyptian Rural Development Company (NERD), a government entity, offered approximately 30,000 feddans east of the Siwa Oasis for reclamation by companies and beneficiaries in 2018. This offering constituted the second phase of the project, encompassing nine areas across the Western Desert, totaling 490,000 feddans irrigated by groundwater. Despite the government's awareness of the existing agricultural drainage problem affecting the ancient oasis, these lands were included in the reclamation initiative.

In the summer of 2025, the investigative reporter conducted an on-site visit to the Siwa Oasis, gaining firsthand insights through interviews with over 20 indigenous farmers and long-time farm owners. While some communicated in Amazigh and others in Arabic, their shared sentiment conveyed a consistent message: increasing salinity has rendered the water unsuitable, and agricultural drainage has devastated their palm and olive groves.

The Siwa Oasis is situated in the Egyptian Western Desert, approximately 50 kilometers east of the Libyan border and roughly 560 kilometers southwest of Cairo. According to data released by the Central Agency for Public Mobilization and Statistics in 2025, the oasis is home to over 38,000 people. The population speaks Siwa, or Amazigh, and their economy is primarily based on agriculture, particularly the cultivation of dates and olives, facilitated by more than 200 natural springs that flow spontaneously within the oasis, with approximately 80 utilized for drinking or irrigation, known locally as "the Roman Springs."

Siwa's agricultural practices rely on two primary groundwater aquifers: the Miocene aquifer, a surface carbonate aquifer extending to a depth of approximately 150 meters, and the Nubian Sandstone aquifer, a deeper aquifer located at approximately 2,600 meters. The Nubian Sandstone aquifer is characterized by its fresh water, exhibiting a relatively low salinity ranging from 198 to 358 parts per million.

This deep aquifer also serves as the primary source of recharge for the surface carbonate aquifer through upward flow via geological fissures.

Recognizing Siwa's significant biodiversity, natural resources, and historical sites, Prime Ministerial Decree No. 1219 of 2002 designated the area a nature reserve. This rich heritage is reflected in the local population, who has maintained their cultural identity, customs, and traditions.

Ali Shuwairf, a Siwan resident and farmer, shares that as soon as a Siwan child takes their first steps, their father introduces them to swimming in the oasis's springs and lakes. Following that, he brings them to the farm for initial lessons in agriculture, which includes pruning palm trees and harvesting olives. In Siwa, he asserts, everyone is born to be a farmer.

He continues, "Water is the cornerstone of both agriculture and existence in Siwa. In our Siwan dialect, we don't refer to water as 'miyyah' like in Arabic; instead, we call it 'aman' in Amazigh, which translates to security.

Water, or security, is the bedrock of all that we do. Without it, a person cannot live or work. The availability of water signifies the opportunity for work and life, while its scarcity leads to the loss of everything."

A Historical Issue

The agricultural drainage problem in the Siwa Oasis is a long-standing issue. The author of this investigative report compiled a database of news archives spanning from 1991 to 2007. A review of the database reveals that government newspapers published 31 articles addressing the Siwa water crisis, consistently identifying the problem and announcing solutions that were never fully implemented.

In October 1991, Al-Akhbar newspaper (governmental) reported that the Siwa Oasis had been at risk of submergence since 1970 due to uncontrolled well drilling for agricultural expansion. In 1996, President Hosni Mubarak (1981–2011) visited Siwa to inaugurate a development project for the oasis, which included establishing factories for date, olive, and salt production. This initiative spurred investment and attracted investors to reclaim additional land.

President Mubarak also initiated a major project to address the groundwater issue and drain excess water. Al-Akhbar newspaper highlighted this project with the headline "Taming the Raging Groundwater in the Siwa Oasis."

Throughout the Mubarak administration, the government asserted progress in resolving the agricultural drainage problem. In 2001, the completion of the Siwa Agricultural Drainage Project was announced. This project, costing over 150 million Egyptian pounds, involved constructing a drainage network approximately 75 kilometers in length, a drainage station, and drilling 1,200 surface and groundwater wells for agricultural purposes.

Analysis of archival news articles from 1991 to 2007 indicates the prominence of key terms. After excluding prepositions, conjunctions, and common words, "water" appeared most frequently (146 times), followed by "drainage" (76 times), "plan" (39 times), "groundwater" (37 times), and "agriculture" (36 times). "Irrigation" was mentioned 34 times, "problem" 31 times, "wells" 21 times, and "flooding" 24 times. "Salinity" and "springs" were each mentioned ten times.

Water first... the most repeated word in Sewa's news archive

This analysis confirms the government's awareness of the drainage and salinity issues. Despite this awareness, agricultural reclamation projects continued. In 2000, 25,000 feddans were allocated to Egyptian and Saudi investors, followed by an additional 30,000 feddans in 2001.

More than three decades after the initial announcement of a solution, the crisis persists. In 2020, the Siwa Oasis was again a focus of government attention with the allocation of EGP 725 million to a new agricultural drainage project, and the commencement of its initial phase. In August 2023, while visiting Matrouh Governorate, which includes Siwa, President Abdel Fattah el-Sisi (2014–present) remarked that the longstanding crisis had been pending resolution for nearly 35 years. He urged the residents of the oasis present at the forum to preserve the project's outcomes.

The events that unfolded during Mubarak's time were mirrored in later years, as state-controlled media and publications persisted in heralding the declaration of a conclusive solution to the Siwa Oasis dilemma. Nevertheless, these initiatives did little to alleviate the ongoing hardships. The Baghi family's property, along with the historic Siwa, continues to be plagued by saltwater issues, and the lakes formed by the build-up of agricultural runoff persist in growing, further threatening the ancient lands.

In April 2023, the severity of the situation was further highlighted by the collapse of an earthen bridge in the Bahey El-Din area due to rising water levels, resulting in the submersion of homes and agricultural lands. According to the then-Member of Parliament, Samira El-Gazzar, the collapse inflicted substantial damage on the village's agricultural sector and residential properties.

The current governmental strategy to mitigate the crisis involves the centralization of agricultural drainage water collection and its subsequent transportation outside the oasis via a 34-kilometer canal to a low-lying area known as Ain Ganbi (locally referred to as Ain Gambi).

However, Professor Mustafa Barsim, a geophysics expert at the Desert Research Center, argues that "the current government project designed to address the agricultural drainage crisis in Siwa suffers from a flawed design and does not offer a sustainable solution, merely delaying the inevitable recurrence of the problem."

He posits that the inevitable seepage of excess water back into the groundwater system will perpetuate the cycle. Professor Barsim emphasizes that a comprehensive solution necessitates the removal of excess water away from the oasis, either through elevation over the northern plateau towards the northern coast or by channeling it southward toward the Dakhla and Kharga oases depressions. He acknowledges that the latter solution would involve significant costs due to the requirement of lifting the water more than 80 meters.

Dr. Ahmed Abboud, a professor of physical geography at Fayoum University, concurs with the assessment that the current project lacks a radical solution. He states, "As long as the water remains within the depression, it will naturally return to its lowest point."

Dr. Abboud also highlights the interconnectedness of Siwa's ecosystem, asserting that alterations in water consumption and drainage patterns have repercussions for all ecosystem components. He concludes, "Similar to the delicate balance within natural food chains, excessive water consumption lies at the core of the problem. Unless we effectively manage water withdrawals, no project can achieve lasting success, as the entire system will invariably be affected."

An Ongoing Crisis

With the assistance of a research team specializing in geographic information systems (GIS) and satellite imagery, we analyzed six atellite images, captured between 1975 and 2025 at ten-year intervals, which demonstrated a significant expansion of water bodies in Siwa. Drainage and salt lakes have collectively increased by 116.04%. Specifically, the area encompassed by the four drainage lakes has grown by 35.12% during the observed period, while salt lakes have expanded by 322%.

Land surface changes between 1975 and 2025

This expansion has resulted in substantial agricultural losses. Old farm areas have contracted by approximately 52.13%, decreasing from 30.6 square kilometers in 1975 to 14.63 square kilometers in 2025.

Omar Baghi attributes the annually rising lake levels to the proliferation of wells associated with land reclamation efforts. He stated, "The widespread availability of drilling equipment has led to an uncontrolled increase in the number of wells, as each farmer and investor seeks independent water sources, without adequate regulatory oversight."

Data from geographic information systems indicate that reclaimed land in the Siwa Oasis has increased fourfold, approximately 274.37%, over the past half-century, expanding from 8.9 square kilometers in 1975 to 33.24 square kilometers in 2025.

A review of the administrative divisions within the Siwa Oasis reveals a marked decline in traditional agricultural practices in several sheikhdoms/villages. Specifically, Umm al-Saghir and al-Maraqi have experienced a near-total loss of cultivated land. This trend is further exemplified by Bahi al-Din, where cultivated land diminished from 2.46 square kilometers to a mere 0.04 square kilometers, representing a 98.4% reduction.

Agurmi witnessed a substantial contraction in traditional agricultural area, decreasing from 3.08 square kilometers to 0.60 square kilometers, an 80.5% decrease. Abu Sharouf-al-Kafrawi also experienced a significant decline, with cultivated land decreasing from 14.23 square kilometers to 6.81 square kilometers, a 52.1% decrease. Siwa City demonstrated the most resilience, yet still experienced a 20% reduction, with cultivated land decreasing from 8.98 square kilometers to 7.18 square kilometers.

Conversely, reclaimed agriculture has seen considerable expansion, particularly in the Umm al-Saghir Oasis, located approximately 270 kilometers from Siwa. Reclaimed land in this area increased from 0.01 square kilometers in 1975 to 1.24 square kilometers in 2025, a 120-fold increase over half a century, positioning it as one of the fastest-growing reclaimed areas in the region.

In Abu Sharouf-Kafrawi, reclaimed land increased from less than one square kilometer in 1975 to approximately 12.9 square kilometers in 2025, a 13-fold increase. This area accounts for approximately 39% of the total reclaimed land in Siwa Oasis over the past half-century, establishing the area northeast of the oasis as a primary driver in the transformation of the oasis' agricultural landscape.

Al-Maraqi experienced an increase in reclaimed land from 0.34 square kilometers to 1.39 square kilometers, a four-fold increase over the past half-century. Agurmi more than tripled its reclaimed area, increasing from 1 square kilometer to 3.38 square kilometers. Bahi El Din also saw significant growth in reclaimed land, nearly tripling from 1.41 square kilometers to 4.45 square kilometers.

Half a century of changes: The progress of land reclamation in Egypt between 1975 and 2025

A study published in 2020, "Soil Salinity Mapping and Water Quality Assessment in the Siwa Oasis Using Geographic Information Systems," corroborates these findings. The study indicated that newly reclaimed land is primarily located on the periphery of the Siwa Oasis, particularly in the elevated regions, owing to the relative ease of land reclamation in these areas compared to those closer to the lake.

Spatial maps from 1992, 2002, and 2015 demonstrate a notable increase in agricultural land cover, with expansion concentrated in the central and eastern sectors of the oasis. Conversely, the northwestern region, proximal to Lake Siwa, experienced an increase in inundated areas resulting from inadequate drainage and intensive irrigation practices. These previously wet sabkhas have undergone transformation into waterlogged terrains. Concurrently, the area of flooded land expanded by approximately 33 square kilometers, constituting 5.4% of the total oasis area by 2015. The saline area also expanded by roughly 29 square kilometers, encompassing over 10% of the oasis.

Dr. Abdel Hamid El-Naggar, Professor of Soil Science at the Faculty of Agriculture, Mansoura University, and co-author of the aforementioned study, attributes these outcomes to water accumulation in low-lying areas, leading to waterlogging and subsequent soil degradation. This process, known as "soil drumming," impairs the soil's agricultural productivity and accelerates salinization. Dr. El-Naggar further notes the transformation of numerous Siwa sabkhas into extensive lakes over time.

Changes in vegetation cover between 1975 and 2025

We conducted analysis of satellite imagery using the Normalized Difference Water Index (NDWI) and the Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI) to identify waterlogged lands and monitor their temporal dynamics. The analysis revealed a 127.8% increase in the area of these lands between 1975 and 2025, attributable to unplanned agricultural expansion and elevated well pumping rates. Consequently, substantial agricultural areas have been rendered unproductive due to waterlogging.

Who should be held responsible?

Since the late 1970s, land acquisition and sales in the Siwa Oasis have evolved through various methods. Initially, some investors procured land directly from the state and subsequently through the New Egyptian Countryside Development Company (NECDC). However, a more prevalent and cost-effective approach, known as the "squatting" method, has emerged. Small-scale investors (under 50 acres) and medium-sized investors (50 acres or more) acquire "squatting rights" from local tribal sheikhs, who traditionally divided the land. Despite the illegality of these transactions, it is widely acknowledged by all parties involved, as noted by environmental lawyer Ahmad Al-Saidi.

Once investors start cultivating land, they subsequently file an official request with the state to legitimize their ownership for a designated fee, a procedure referred to in Egypt as the "legalization of squatter lands." The government periodically opens its doors to accept these requests, only to discover that the same parcels of land have been sold twice: first by local authorities, and then by the state.

Legislation has been enacted to regulate the unauthorized occupation of state lands, commencing with Law No. 144 of 2017, which was later superseded by Law No. 168 of 2025, promulgated on August 13, 2025. This law is designed to process requests and grievances related to the legalization of squatter lands, which are then submitted to newly formed review committees. Furthermore, it permits the resubmission of grievances and the submission of new requests for legalization, even if previously denied.

While in Siwa, the investigative reporter sought to understand the truth behind land ownership and the selling process. She observed that the procedure is quite straightforward, leading some locals and outsiders to take on the role of "brokers" to facilitate transactions between buyers and sellers. The cost of an acre varies based on its location: land nearer to the city center commands a higher price (typically owned by oasis residents), while prices tend to drop as you venture toward the outskirts (often held by corporations and investors).

Notably, older land parcels have experienced price appreciation, driven by their conversion into hotels, tourist facilities, and development projects—a strategy employed by local residents to mitigate drought and desertification. A local guide and broker stated that land prices can begin at 1,000 Egyptian pounds ($21) approximately 40 kilometers from the city center, with average prices in slightly more distant areas around 70,000 Egyptian pounds ($1,464). In the heart of the old town, the price of an acre can reach four to five million Egyptian pounds ($83,680 to $104,600).

Land sales facilitated by the state have increased in recent years, particularly since 2014 through the NECDC, a government entity coordinating between the Ministries of Housing, Agriculture, and Irrigation. As part of the government's 1.5 Million Feddan Project, thirty thousand acres in eastern Siwa have been allocated for sale and usufruct. Purchases are restricted to areas of 1,000 feddans (400 hectares) or greater, with a maximum of 23 investors permitted to share the land.

The writer of this investigative report established a comprehensive database featuring the leading companies active in Siwa that possess extensive land holdings. This information was compiled from news archives and accounts provided by local inhabitants. Furthermore, this database was compared with a national database of companies, relying on official government statistics.

Public sector Vs private sector who owns the land?

The database comprises thirteen companies, the largest of which is the NECDC, which reclaimed approximately 19,779 acres in 2024, along with cultivating an additional 7,000 acres. Subsidiaries operating under this umbrella include "Siwa Earth," which reclaimed approximately 150 acres in 2024, following reclamation of only 20 acres in 2023, and leveled 65 acres of sandy land at a cost of 1.1 million Egyptian pounds (US$23,000) in 2024. Similar activity is observed with "Knooz Siwa," which commenced operations in 2021 with a total area of ​​2,235 acres, and "Al-Ashraf," which covers an area of ​​2,500 acres.

In November 2024, the NECDC divested approximately 15,000 acres in the Farafra and Siwa extensions to a subsidiary of El Sewedy Capital. Previously, in 2018, an agreement was reached to sell an additional 30,000 acres to a Spanish/Egyptian investor, whose identity has not been disclosed in official publications, for the cultivation of olive trees and the establishment of an olive oil extraction facility in Siwa. Attempts to identify this investor by the author of this report were unsuccessful, as local Siwa residents possessed limited information regarding the individual.

Since the dawn of the millennium, several private enterprises have emerged, including Siwa Palm, Dirco, Express, Al-Nouran, Al-Nasr, Al-Ashraf, Mada'in Siwa, and Zahrat El-Delta. These companies engage in the cultivation of diverse crops, with a particular emphasis on palm trees. Notably, some have introduced novel varieties, specifically Saudi cultivars such as the "Medjool," intended for export and actively promoted by entities like Mada'in Siwa and Beshaer Siwa. The latter offers an investment model based on an "owners' association" framework, branded as the "Union of Investors and Farmers in the Siwa Oasis," as detailed on its corporate website.

These companies' operations extend beyond agricultural production, encompassing land reclamation and its subsequent sale to smaller-scale investors. Their Facebook pages feature numerous advertisements promoting agricultural opportunities and investment prospects within the Siwa Oasis.

A 2025 research publication indicates that, by 2024, approximately 70% of farmers in the Siwa Oasis had adopted solar energy for irrigation purposes, predominantly sourced from large investment firms situated on the periphery of the oasis. Consequently, solar energy has become a critical enabler for the continued extraction of substantial volumes of fossil water from the Nubian Aquifer, thereby exacerbating the submersion of the oasis center.

The study further notes that, by 2024, Siwa had witnessed the establishment of numerous retail outlets specializing in the sale and maintenance of solar equipment, alongside irrigation stations with capacities ranging from 70 to 100 horsepower. The smallest commercially available plant proved sufficient to irrigate between 30 and 50 acres, thereby shifting the economic paradigm in favor of large-scale projects at the expense of smaller agricultural holdings. With land values remaining comparatively low, the market has become conducive to the entry of medium and large-scale investors.

Increasing Salinity

Knooz Siwa, an NECDC subsidiary involved in the new project, cultivates both water-intensive field crops (wheat, barley, corn, and fava beans) and traditional crops (palm and olive trees) in the Siwa Oasis. This is facilitated by a low-salinity (200 ppm) well, 1,050 meters deep, powered by solar energy, and yielding approximately 500 cubic meters per hour. However, local farmer Issa Khater, a 35-year-old Siwan, struggles to irrigate his inherited land in central Siwa due to increasing water salinity.

The initial sight of Issa Khater's ancestral land reveals a stark landscape: lifeless palm and olive trees, and barren soil. At the very moment you set foot on his land, dry thorns snag your clothing and only withered vegetation remains underfoot.

Issa Khater recounts, "From the age of seven, I accompanied my father to this land. It was once a flourishing garden, a paradise of olive trees, palm trees, pomegranates, apples, and grapes. The landscape was vibrant and verdant. It truly felt like entering paradise."

Khater attributes his agricultural challenges to increased water salinity. Khater's main issue is the water's salinity. In his discussion with the reporter, he mentioned that they originally depended on water sourced from older wells, until a government directive was issued to drill new wells (a recurring action we have recorded in the news archives).

He explains, "These wells initially provided adequate water, but after ten to fifteen years, the water became brackish and saline, leading to the complete decline of agriculture. Consequently, no crops have grown on this land for fifteen years. The orchards, once our primary source of income and painstakingly cultivated by our forefathers, have withered. We inherited this legacy but were unable to sustain it. The water's degradation ruined everything, and farming as they once did became impossible."

A 2023 study, co-authored by a team of researchers, investigated groundwater quality in the Siwa Oasis, providing a scientific analysis of the impact of new well drilling on the oasis's water resources. The study analyzed 106 water samples collected in January 2022, processed at the Desert Research Center laboratory of the Ministry of Agriculture and Land Reclamation according to established standard procedures. Results indicated that groundwater salinization is primarily attributable to dissolution and ion exchange processes, with calcium, chloride, magnesium, sulfate, and sodium ions identified as the most significant contributors to increased salinity levels.

The study determined that a majority (79%) of private wells contain slightly saline water, exhibiting salinity concentrations ranging from 1,552 to 4,385 mg/L. Only 21% of private wells contained relatively fresh water, with lower salinity levels (504–1,496 mg/L).

The situation is more pronounced in government-managed wells, where 80% of the aquifer exhibits saline water conditions, and the remaining 20% displays highly saline water (5011–6693 mg/L). Notably, no fresh water was detected in these wells.

Dr. Saad Muhalal, Professor of Water Sciences at the Desert Research Center and a lead researcher on the study, attributes the elevated salinity levels to the traditional practice of farmers and local residents drilling shallow wells into the Miocene carbonate aquifer. This aquifer, while closest to the surface, is inherently more saline. The relative ease of extraction, requiring only 10 to 20 meters of depth for water flow, has contributed to its widespread use. However, Dr. Muhalal emphasizes that the haphazard drilling practices, lacking proper engineering design, are a primary cause for concern.

He further explains: "This unregulated drilling has led to a critical issue: the mixing of freshwater from the deep Nubian aquifer—a strategic national reserve—with the brackish water from the upper Miocene aquifer. This mixing process is actively degrading the overall water quality due to the proliferation of poorly constructed wells drilled by farmers." Dr. Muhalal characterizes the situation as an utter catastrophe for farmers, stating that the lack of proper well insulation and piping results in the re-introduction of extracted water back into the aquifer, leading to further salinization of both the well and the surrounding land.

A 2022 study, "Groundwater Quality in the Siwa Oasis: Factors Controlling Spatial and Temporal Variations," revealed that 61% of groundwater samples exceeded safe salinity limits for irrigation, making them unsuitable and adversely affecting crop yields.

The study documented total dissolved solids, commonly referred to as salinity, ranging from 1,367 to 8,645 mg/L, with the highest concentration (5,432.5 mg/L) observed in approximately 25% of samples from central Siwa (the Old Area). Throughout the duration of the study, a gradual rise in salinity levels was noted, escalating from an average of 3,754.3 mg/L in 2014 to 4,222.4 mg/L by 2020.

The research underscores salinity as a crucial determinant of irrigation water quality. Electrical conductivity (EC) values in most samples surpassed the permissible threshold for irrigation (2250 microsiemens/cm), indicating that the high salinity levels pose a threat to most crops, excluding salt-tolerant varieties.

Issa shared the land with his five sisters, but when it became tainted by salinity, he had no choice but to leave it behind and pursue a different career: plastering and construction. He is now involved in building and painting homes and factories. He expresses, "I require income to support my family, and the land can no longer deliver that." His sisters, who once thrived by harvesting olives and dates for sale or personal use, now find themselves, after the loss of their husbands, depending on basic manual work within their homes to care for their children.

Dr. Abdul Hamid al-Najjar highlights that soil salinity significantly contributes to the reduction of agricultural output, diminishing from 75% to 50% and then to 25% as salinity levels rise, ultimately resulting in zero productivity at a critical threshold for certain crops. Although olives show a certain degree of resilience, persistent salinity, along with inadequate drainage and waterlogging (flooding), results in soil deterioration and a total loss of productivity.

Sparkling Wells

Amr Baghi identifies the uncontrolled nature of artesian wells, locally known as "sparkling wells," as a contributing factor to the waterlogging issue. These wells flow naturally, and both investors and farmers in Siwa are reluctant to seal them, Baghi shared with the reporter.

Baghi elaborates, “Siwa's investors and farmers traditionally avoid closing these wells due to a belief that it diminishes their flow over time. Consequently, many wells remain continuously open. While some farmers utilize these wells for temporary water storage during off-planting hours, many others allow excess irrigation water to drain away, leading to continuous operation of numerous artesian wells. If we consider that just ten farmers engage in this practice, that results in ten sparkling wells running non-stop, 24 hours a day, which inevitably causes a substantial increase in the water level."

He mentions, "Maybe numerous individuals are unaware that the lake features wells functioning around the clock. At the far end of the lost lands, there exists a sparkling well." He highlights that the ten acres he lost due to the lake's expansion includes a sparkling well, which plays a role in augmenting the lake's water supply.

Dr. Mustafa Barsim emphasizes that a crucial aspect of land reclamation initiatives involves drilling wells at different depths to access groundwater for irrigation purposes. However, Saad Muhalal argues that the core issue lies with the older, privately-owned wells. Many of these are artesian wells and operate without proper engineering insulation, in stark contrast to the wells established by newer companies, which are expected to adhere to contemporary standards that mitigate such problems. Despite this claim, social media evidence uncovered a video clip linked to several of the new reclamation companies showing that they too have artesian wells.

Dr. Barsim advocates for regulating the haphazard drilling of wells until scientifically sound alternatives are identified. He also proposes repurposing the artesian water for small-scale initiatives, such as fish farming, to mitigate its accumulation within the oasis and alleviate the crisis.

The agricultural drainage crisis and ensuing salinity issues impact numerous Siwa residents, including Ali Shuwairf, Haidar Abdel Salam, Sayed Momia, and Khalifa al-Tunisi, in addition to Amr Baghi and Issa Khater, all of whom are grappling with the challenges posed by land submergence, increased salinity, and diminishing soil fertility.

While the government continues to dig canals and pump out agricultural drainage as part of its new project, the Baghi family sits on the shores of Lake Siwa, contemplating the acres swallowed by the water and wondering about the future of their farm on a winter night buffeted by winds and storms as the lake expands. Meanwhile, Issa wakes up at dawn, searching for a livelihood away from the abandoned land. His sisters stay home, embroidering clothes, hoping to save a few quid to support their families after their inheritance became fallow land. Meanwhile, investors continue to reclaim and cultivate the land using deep well water, unconcerned with the additional burden this places on the old farmers.
