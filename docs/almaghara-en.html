By: Eman Mounir

This investigation is the third part of the series “Green Paradise Illusion.” It was produced in partnership with the Bertha Foundation (2025), during which Eman Mounir was a Bertha Challenge Fellow.

In September 2022, Ihab and Muhammad Alaa El-Din encountered a severe setback on their newly reclaimed farm in the Al-Maghra Oasis, located 400 kilometers west of Cairo in the Western Desert. Palm trees planted six months prior had perished due to excessive saline irrigation water.

This incident compounded numerous prior attempts to cultivate the land, which their late father acquired in 2018 as a usufruct right 2 from the New Egyptian Countryside Development Company (NECDC). This acquisition was part of the government's 1.5 Million Feddan Project, an initiative designed to reclaim desert land, expand agricultural acreage, and enhance national food security.

Their father, who passed away in 2021 from coronavirus, dreamt of a verdant landscape. His sons recall his desire "to see green as far as his eyes could see," a dream that died with him. They believe he contracted the virus at an Al-Maghra Lands Companies Alliance meeting where others were also infected.

When the Alaa El-Din family acquired the farm in 2018, their initial step was to drill a well to access groundwater, eager to commence farming without delay. They submitted a water sample for testing, but the findings were alarming: the salinity level soared to 4,500 parts per million (ppm), exceeding the recommended irrigation threshold of 1,500 ppm by threefold.

“We had bad luck,” the son says with a heavy heart. “The combination of poor soil and water issues made agriculture nearly unfeasible, and no one warned us about the salinity crisis affecting the water.” Despite this setback, they felt they couldn't retreat after making the initial payments. The father sought guidance from scientists, hoping to identify remediation strategies for the soil and reduce salinity levels.

Regrettably, as time passed, conditions deteriorated—the salinity problem intensified, reaching 7,500 ppm. This escalation transformed farming into a challenging endeavor that demanded significant financial investment merely to sustain plant life.

Alaa El-Din and his sons are not the only ones affected; over a thousand companies benefiting from Al-Maghra Lands also struggle with high salinity. This problem causes significant financial losses due to increased labor and production costs, limited crop options, and reduced productivity per acre. Many beneficiaries interviewed, along with those whose grievances were documented, claimed they were unaware of the salinity problem before signing agreements, a claim disputed by the NECDC.

In this report, the third phase of the "Green Paradise Illusion" project, we investigate the agricultural reclamation efforts in the Al-Maghra desert area, which is a key component of the government's 1.5 million feddan initiative. Using geographic information systems (GIS) and on-site observations, the study reveals that the water quality in the majority of groundwater wells in Al-Maghra fails to meet established irrigation standards.

This study further uncovers a significant deficiency in transparency during the land sale process to small investors, indicating that farmers did not receive comprehensive details regarding the characteristics of the land. Following extensive examinations of advertisements, proposals, specifications, and contracts, along with nearly ten salinity assessments, it is evident that small-scale Egyptian investors—lacking adequate knowledge—acquired land from the government that featured highly saline irrigation sources and was inappropriate for agricultural use. After unsuccessful attempts at farming, some have shifted to aquaculture, thereby intensifying the strain on water resources in the arid region.

Law No. 143 of 1981, pertaining to desert lands, applies to the Al-Maghra lands. This law designates the General Authority for Reconstruction Projects and Agricultural Development as the governing body responsible for desert lands allocated for reclamation and cultivation. It is important to note that the Authority, along with the New Urban Communities Authority and the Ministry of Finance, are government shareholders in the NECDC, the project's primary developer. The company's capital is eight billion Egyptian pounds.

Established by Cabinet Resolution No. (6) on November 4, 2015, the NECDC is an Egyptian joint-stock company formed to spearhead the 1.5 million-acre reclamation and development project launched in December 2015. Its board comprises three sitting ministers and six seasoned members. The NECDC is mandated to allocate lands designated for agricultural reclamation to beneficiaries, encompassing both small-scale and large-scale investors, within the project's four million-acre scope.

Article 6

The Authority shall plan, study, and research land reclamation and cultivation projects under this law. It shall implement, supervise, and follow up on these projects, ensuring, by all scientific means, their suitability for reclamation and agricultural and economic use, and the feasibility of irrigation from groundwater, surface water, treated wastewater, or other water resources. The Authority shall act through its agencies or contract with qualified individuals, companies, and local or foreign experts, in accordance with applicable regulations.

The events in the Al-Maghra lands constituted a violation of the governing law, its executive regulations, and its interpretive memorandum. Specifically, beneficiaries encountered unforeseen obstacles in reclaiming and cultivating land acquired from the NECDC, stemming from elevated salinity levels and substandard soil quality in certain sectors.

Saltwater Reclamation

In December 2015 4 , the government announced the commencement of the 1.5 Million Feddan Project, a key agricultural development initiative within the government's economic reform agenda for the preceding decade. This project seeks to enhance food security, establish sustainable urban communities, and generate employment opportunities for young people through the allocation of desert lands for agricultural reclamation.

The project's implementation was structured in three phases, encompassing nine principal areas within the Western Desert. Among these is the Al-Maghra Oasis, situated in a natural depression spanning approximately 1,600 square kilometers. The oasis's topography, ranging from 50 meters above sea level to 50 meters below sea level, presents a distinctive yet delicate geological context. Its location is approximately 70 kilometers from El Alamein and 300 kilometers from Alexandria, with connectivity to these urban centers facilitated by several unpaved roadways.

Alaa El-Din and his neighbors discovered the chance to purchase land in Al-Maghra through various advertisements placed by the company in newspapers and official websites at that time. They promptly submitted their applications, although the process was quite challenging. The requirements specified that applications could only be submitted by companies. Further requirements included applicant companies possessing capital equivalent to 25% of the land's assessed value, a 20% down payment, and subsequent installment payments spread over nine to ten years, subject to an annual decreasing interest rate of 11% for small investors. It's important to note that specific conditions may vary based on the investment category. A "usufruct" contract, incorporating alternative terms, was also presented as an option.

The Al-Maghra Oasis reclamation project encompasses approximately 280,000 feddans (1,176 square kilometers), offered in three phases between 2017 and 2020. A fourth phase focused on infrastructure development without additional land sales. Initial land prices were EGP 18,000 per feddan (approximately $1,000 in 2017), increasing to EGP 23,000 (approximately $1,461 in 2020) based on factors such as water well availability. Alaa El-Din secured a 220-feddan usufruct contract in the second phase of the Al-Maghra project, following an unsuccessful first application. He invested around EGP 4.4 million (approximately $275,000 at that time) — a significant amount by Egyptian norms, exceeding the country's average annual income per capita by over a hundredfold.

To grasp the changes that have taken place in Al-Maghra, we collaborated with a researcher expert in geographic information systems (GIS) to examine satellite imagery from 2009 to 2025. This analysis involved five satellite images spanning multiple years, utilizing advanced software tools like ArcGIS and Google Earth to monitor agricultural development, salinity levels, vegetation cover, and various other characteristics of the Al-Maghra region.

Analysis revealed a nineteen-fold expansion of reclaimed areas from 31.2 square kilometers in 2009 to 604.8 square kilometers by 2025, representing a 64% increase since the project's commencement in 2019.

Dr. Ahmad Fawzy Diab, a water strategy expert at the United Nations and professor of water resources at the Desert Research Center, notes, "Prior to the project, numerous informal settlements existed in Al-Maghra, which were subsequently formalized and integrated into the project." This is corroborated by pre-existing reclaimed agricultural areas visible in 2009 imagery and the NECDC's initiative to legalize informal land claims within the 1.5 million-acre reclamation project area.

Soil Salinity Change in the Al-Maghra Region (2009–2025)

We also conducted an analysis of soil salinity in Al-Maghra using the Salinity Index, which integrates specific spectral bands (red, near-infrared, and short-wavelength) to differentiate saline from non-saline soils. The Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI) was also measured to assess the impact of salinity on vegetation health.

We observed substantial shifts in salinity distribution concurrent with increased land reclamation efforts..Highly saline soils exhibited a 1,400% increase, moderately saline soils increased by approximately 3,677%, and slightly saline soils increased by 18.8%. Conversely, non-saline soil area decreased significantly from 602.9 km² in 2009 to 343.11 km² in 2025, representing a reduction of approximately 43%. This indicates a loss of over 250 km² of non-saline land within a 16-year period.

How Did Vegetation Cover Change in Al-Maghra Over 16 Years?

Significant alterations in vegetation cover area and type were also observed. Dense vegetation cover increased from 1.97 km² in 2009 to 3.34 km² in 2025.

Intermediate vegetation cover experienced a notable increase after 2009, rising from 1.16 km² to over 743 km² in 2019, followed by a sharp decline to less than 39 km² in 2025. Meager vegetation cover decreased from over 1,069 km² in 2009 to 325 km² in 2019, before increasing again to approximately 650 km² in 2025. Notably, derelict vegetation cover doubled from less than 0.2 km² in 2009 to over 381 km² in 2025.

The Al-Maghra Oasis also experienced a substantial increase in built-up area during the same period, growing by 380% from 1.04 km² to 5 km², as the responsible company allocated 2% of the land area for infrastructure and building construction.

A study conducted by Adel Abdel Hamid Alwan, Professor of Soil Sciences at the Desert Research Center, assessed the agricultural suitability of 128,000 acres in Al-Maghra. The findings indicated that 48% of the surveyed land is entirely unsuitable for agricultural production.

Professor Alwan attributes this unsuitability not only to the saline irrigation water in Al-Maghra but also to inherent soil characteristics. The soil's loamy texture and low permeability impede water infiltration, leading to salt accumulation within the root zone (1.5 to 2 meters deep). This concentrated salt exposure poses a significant challenge to plant growth.

He further explains that Al-Maghra's geological history as a former Nile River estuary has resulted in a complex soil composition. Successive layers of surface sand overlie an original clay layer, a product of erosion and liquefaction. This stratified structure exacerbates agricultural difficulties, as the clay layer obstructs salt drainage to deeper soil levels.

Water Well Salinity

Alaa El-Din's farm, typical of the region, utilizes an underground well extending to a depth of 195.5 meters to extract approximately 1,500 cubic meters of water daily for irrigation. Alaa El-Din independently financed the well's construction, as not all farms provided by the NECDC included pre-existing water wells.

The well is connected to the Al-Maghra Aquifer, which spans from the western edge of the Nile Delta to the east of the Qattara Depression. The aquifer's thickness varies from 50 meters in the east to 900 meters in the west. The aquifer's water is a combination of ancient water in the west and renewable water in the east. According to a 2019 study by the Egyptian Desalination Research Center of Excellence (affiliated with the Ministry of Agriculture and Land Reclamation), the total groundwater salinity within this basin ranges from 1,000 parts per million in the east to 20,000 parts per million at the Qattara Depression in the west.

Dr. Ahmad Fawzy Diab, a United Nations water strategy expert and Professor of Water Resources at the Desert Research Center, notes that water salinity in Al-Maghra varies with depth. The first and second layers exhibit salinity levels below 4,000 parts per million. However, salinity increases with extraction depth, ranging from 5,000 to 20,000 parts per million, rendering it suitable only for salt-tolerant plant species.

The fluctuation in ground level relative to sea level in Al-Maghra contributes to variations in the salinity of well water, a phenomenon corroborated by Professor Muhammad Galal El-Tarabili of Port Said University's Faculty of Engineering, based on his analysis of digital maps of the region.

The reporter investigated water salinity in Al-Maghra, personally experiencing the extreme salinity of water from certain wells. In addition to visiting Alaa El-Din's farm, the reporter interviewed several farm owners, consulted with researchers, reviewed well water analysis results and peer-reviewed scientific studies, and monitored numerous complaints on social media to gain a comprehensive understanding of the problem.

Muhammad Alaa El-Din explains, "Our primary challenge is the high salinity, which forces us to implement various procedures and spend extra money to mitigate its effects on our plants. Unfortunately, this sometimes leads to fungal infections in the roots of trees, resulting in their death. We sometimes catch the problem early, but other times we lose the tree entirely."

He continues, "Initially, due to our lack of experience, we lost many trees. However, over time, we learned how to manage these challenges and began collaborating with specialists who introduced chemical agents and components to mitigate soil salinity. Despite our efforts, the effects are still present, although we have managed to save some trees. Still, the costs remain extremely high; a plant would typically cost much less under normal circumstances, but for us, the expenses increase significantly due to the additional efforts required to combat salinity."

A 2021 study published in Springer Nature by researchers Muhammad El-Tarabili and Hossam El-Din El-Moghazi, titled "GIS-Based Assessment and Statistical Identification of Groundwater Geochemistry for Potential Irrigation Use in Al-Maghra, Egypt," assessed the suitability of groundwater for irrigation purposes. The study involved a geochemical analysis of 46 groundwater samples collected during deep well drilling in 2018 and 2019, covering an area of 230,000 acres.

The research concluded that approximately 25% of the groundwater within the project area is unsuitable for irrigation due to its excessive hardness and a magnesium risk exceeding 50%. Furthermore, crop classification based on electrical conductivity indicated that only halophytic and highly salt-tolerant crops could survive if the groundwater is used for irrigation without prior treatment.

Muhammad El-Tarabili, co-author of the study and professor at the Faculty of Engineering at Port Said University, highlights the risks associated with elevated magnesium levels. He explains that high magnesium concentrations are often accompanied by high chloride concentrations. While plants exhibit some tolerance to chloride, magnesium introduces an additional detrimental effect. Beyond increasing salinity, magnesium impairs the plant's ability to absorb water

Professor El-Tarabili elaborates that although water may be present in the soil, elevated magnesium levels increase the osmotic pressure. This requires the plant to expend more energy to extract water, and in many instances, the plant is unable to do so, preventing it from accessing water and essential nutrients like nitrogen, potassium, and calcium.

The reporter collected ten analysis reports from water samples between 2020 and 2025 from the Alaa El Din farm and Al-Maghra Land farm revealed that the water was unsuitable for irrigation. Salinity levels in samples from Al-Maghra Land farm ranged from 4,952 parts per million (ppm) to 5,096 ppm, while samples from Alaa El-Din farm recorded 7,608 ppm in 2023 and 7,568 ppm in 2024

The reports also indicated an increasing absorbed sodium ratio (SAR), a key indicator of declining water quality. At the Alaa El-Din farm, the SAR reached 19.76 ppm in 2023 before decreasing to 9.28 ppm in 2024. At Al-Maghra Land, the SAR was recorded at 8.51 ppm in 2020 and increased to 16.22 ppm in 2025

Amr Salah, an assistant professor in the Olive and Semi-Arid Fruit Research Department at the Horticulture Research Institute, aided the investigator in interpreting sample analysis reports. He clarified that water salinity comprises both beneficial and detrimental salts. Beneficial salts contribute positively to soil health and diminish the need for fertilization, whereas harmful salts, particularly sodium, present a considerable threat. Sodium, when combined with chlorine, forms salt that weakens soil structure, induces cell damage (plasmolysis), and precipitates "physiological thirst," thereby hindering the trees' capacity to absorb adequate water.

Professor Salah further stated that elevated test results are explicitly noted in the report, thereby absolving the institute of responsibility regarding the water's utilization. He specified that fruit crops, including olives and palm trees, can tolerate irrigation with water exhibiting salinity levels not exceeding 1,500–2,000 parts per million. Consequently, high salinity levels exert adverse effects on both the soil and the resulting produce.

Alaa El Din Farm owners attempted to mitigate the salinity issue by introducing new plant varieties, such as cabbage, lettuce, tomatoes, and eggplant; however, these efforts proved unsuccessful, resulting in substantial financial losses and necessitating the sale of portions of the property to sustain the farm's operations. Only specific varieties of olive and palm trees demonstrated resilience.

Ihab Alaa El-Din stated, "The severe salinity in this area necessitates significant investment. We were compelled to sell four or five plots of land to maintain the farm. Furthermore, we hold the largest share in the company, which we were required to establish as a condition set by the NECDC for land utilization".

He added, "We consider each tree a vital part of our family. The illness or death of a tree is felt as deeply as if one of our children were afflicted".

Abdullah Sarhan, an agricultural engineer at Alaa El-Din Farm, noted, "Salinity not only restricts the diversity of cultivable plants but also negatively affects the health of trees, leading to leaf drop and root rot. This, in turn, reduces per-acre productivity and results in significant financial losses".

Researcher Amr Salah indicated that oil-bearing varieties—such as jojoba, jatropha, and canola—are most suitable for Al-Maghra soils, alongside palm trees and olives. He emphasized that fully oil-bearing varieties like Karantina and Maraki are optimal choices, producing high oil content and superior quality.

A consortium of 20 Al-Maghra companies, encompassing a total land area of 4,760 acres, consulted the Desert Research Center to formulate an integrated agricultural plan to address the salinity challenge. The plan categorized the soil into medium-productivity, low-productivity, and very-low-productivity classifications based on its suitability for cultivating six crops. The center advocated for the cultivation of the oil-bearing jatropha and the fodder plant Bonicam, citing their adaptability to harsh conditions and tolerance of salinity.

Following expert consultation, the Alaa El-Din family implemented a pond system for water storage and saltwater mullet aquaculture. This initiative, however, proved unsuccessful, resulting in fish mortality. Muhammad Alaa El-Din explained that the ponds were intended to mitigate water salinity through sedimentation and to serve as a supplementary water source in the event of well failure. The primary challenge encountered was the inability to effectively balance the distinct requirements of agricultural irrigation and aquaculture, particularly concerning water drainage management.

Muhammad Alaa El-Din identified the core issue as the difficulty in harmonizing the water demands of the farm with those of the fish. He elaborated, "Discrepancies arose between irrigation needs and pond drainage capacity, especially during periods of fish hibernation or elevated ammonia levels. The necessity for immediate water drainage due to ammonia buildup often coincided with periods when the land did not require irrigation, leading to unproductive water disposal. Consequently, the experiment was deemed impractical and discontinued."

While a successful fish farming operation in Al-Maghra could not be identified, GIS analysis indicated a substantial increase in water basins, encompassing both water storage and aquaculture, within the Al-Maghra area. These basins expanded by approximately 187% between 2009 and 2025, with a more moderate increase of 44% observed between 2019 and 2025.

Reservoir Sustainability

Beyond issues of water and soil salinity, Al-Maghra farmers face challenges stemming from governmental reductions in water allocations, which contravene existing contractual agreements.

Muhammad Hegazy, a proprietor and head of the Al-Maghra Companies Alliance, stated, "The contract for his 230-acre land stipulated the drilling of two water wells. The first well had a capacity of 1,798 cubic meters per day, with the second well to be drilled upon 60% land reclamation. Despite fulfilling these conditions and renewing the license for the initial well, the permitted water withdrawal was unexpectedly reduced to 1,500 cubic meters per day, and the license for the second well was denied." He further noted that the reduced water volume from the first well is only adequate for irrigating 100 acres of tree crops, raising concerns about the irrigation and reclamation of the remaining land.

The Alaa El-Din farm faces a similar predicament, prompting consideration of uprooting half of their trees to ensure adequate water supply for the remaining trees. Ihab Alaa El-Din expressed, "The forced removal of established, fruit-bearing trees due to water scarcity is a distressing and psychologically taxing experience. We consider our crops integral to our lives, akin to our children, and their destruction represents a profound loss".

Researchers Ahmed Fawzy Diab and Muhammad El-Tarabily posit that, based on available water resources, the Al-Maghra project has a projected lifespan of only 100 years. Furthermore, the continuous extraction of these resources is contributing to increased salinity levels.

A study published in Nature in 2025 investigated the effects of groundwater extraction and saltwater intrusion on the Al-Maghra aquifer through simulations projecting over a century. Modeling various well withdrawal rates, the research determined that operating 1,000 wells at daily extraction rates ranging from 1,000 to 1,750 cubic meters per well would result in a decline in the aquifer's water level, ranging from 18% to 30% of its total thickness.

Elevated groundwater extraction rates exacerbate seawater intrusion, with the 3,500 mg/L isochlor extending 30.7 to 57.1 km inland across the eastern, central, and western sectors of the aquifer. Modeling suggests that continued extraction could induce a water table decline of 24 to 100 meters over a century, potentially increasing salinity from approximately 2,400 mg/L to over 6,400 mg/L under certain scenarios.

In conclusion, sustained groundwater withdrawal threatens the equilibrium between freshwater and saltwater within the basin, raising substantial concerns regarding water quality degradation and the long-term viability of regional agricultural development.

Hydrogeological analysis of the Al-Maghra area, conducted using Geographic Information Systems (GIS), integrated topographical, geological, and aquifer thickness data. Comparative analysis between 2009 and 2025 indicates a distinct shift in water resource characteristics. Areas exhibiting high water abundance decreased significantly from 1.21 km² to 0.11 km², while areas with medium abundance experienced a marginal increase from 2.74 to 2.92 km². Areas characterized by low abundance remained relatively stable, registering 1,069.49 km² in 2009 and increasing slightly to 1,070.40 km² in 2025.

Who should be held responsible?

Khaled El-Mallah, an agricultural engineer from the Nile Delta, invested his savings accumulated over two decades of working abroad in a plot of land acquired during the initial land auctions. Despite his aspirations of establishing a productive farm and contributing to desert development, salinity issues presented a significant challenge. He stated, "Had I been aware of the land's unproductive potential from the outset, I would not have invested here." Addressing the salinity problem depleted his financial resources, limiting his reclamation efforts to 30 acres.

Safaa Felefel, another participant in the initial offering, reported investing her entire savings in the land and experiencing substantial losses due to plant mortality, with only olive trees surviving. She lamented, "The current yield from the land is insufficient to cover my installment payments to the NECDC, despite having acquired the land eight years ago."

Expert opinions diverge regarding the farmers' prior awareness of the salinity issue before land acquisition. Ahmed Fawzy Diab contends that water salinity has been a known factor for some time. Prior to the 1.5 million feddan project, individuals, often referred to as squatters, had already reclaimed areas in Al-Maghra. Abbas Sharaqi, a professor of geology and resources at Cairo University, attributes the crisis, in part, to the expedited implementation of the project. He stated, "The project was implemented hastily, lacking adequate prior research. Well drilling occurred without sufficient investigation and at excessively close intervals. Consequently, the implementing agency may not have clearly identified salinity levels. These shortcomings subsequently became evident through declining well efficiency and increased salinity."

Beneficiaries have confirmed to investigators that they repeatedly submitted complaints to the NECDC regarding the increasing salinity of water and soil, but received no response. In 2021, they initiated legal action, filing a lawsuit against the company responsible for the project, the Ministry of Agriculture, and the Prime Minister.

One complainant, who wished to remain anonymous, stated that the case had progressed to the appointment of a court expert. Expert committees conducted site visits, assessed the problem, consulted with residents, and produced an official report.

He further commented that reaching the expert stage was a significant achievement, given that the lawsuit named the President of the Republic, the Prime Minister, and the Minister of Agriculture as defendants, due to the project's national importance. He expressed frustration with the protracted process and ineffectual complaints, noting a discrepancy between the reality on the ground and the consistently positive portrayals of the project in official publications.

Environmental lawyer Ahmed Al-Saidi argues that the government has a responsibility to support investors in desert reclamation projects, especially concerning issues like salinity, given the state's initial contractual agreements with investors.

Climate Shock

The problem of soil salinity is exacerbated during the winter months. Rainfall has a detrimental effect on plants in the Al-Maghra area, which are adapted to saline or slightly saline water. Exposure to fresh water causes "salt shock," a phenomenon resulting from the abrupt alteration in salt concentration around plant roots, disrupting osmotic balance and causing stress and damage. When rainwater mixes with the saline soil, salts are deposited directly onto the roots, intensifying the damage.

Khaled al-Mallah, a farmer involved in the Al-Maghra project, explains that rainfall, rather than being beneficial, represents a significant shock to plants due to the sudden change in water quality impacting their roots.

Rainfall data obtained through Geographic Information Systems (GIS) analysis indicates an approximate 494% increase in average rainfall in the Al-Maghra area between 2009 and 2023, with 2021 recording the highest average rainfall at approximately 386 mm.

Almost five years following the death of their father, Alaa El-Din, his children persist in cultivating the land, steadfastly upholding his vision. Despite increased farming costs, their olive trees are yielding small quantities, which they process into a limited supply of oil bearing their father's brand. Ihab states, "Selling the land has never been an option; it represents an honor to me. I feel a responsibility to my father, who I imagine will ask me about the land, the trust he placed in me, and my contribution to providing pure crops for the community."

While Khaled al-Mallah contemplates selling, he has been unable to find buyers. Safaa Fleifel, on the other hand, prefers to avoid loans and continue her work through self-funding. Meanwhile, the residents of Al-Maghra are collectively adapting to challenging circumstances, striving to preserve their aspirations and maintain hope.
