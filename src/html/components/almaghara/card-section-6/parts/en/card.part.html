Who should be held responsible?

Khaled El-Mallah, an agricultural engineer from the Nile Delta, invested his savings accumulated over two decades of working abroad in a plot of land acquired during the initial land auctions. Despite his aspirations of establishing a productive farm and contributing to desert development, salinity issues presented a significant challenge. He stated, "Had I been aware of the land's unproductive potential from the outset, I would not have invested here." Addressing the salinity problem depleted his financial resources, limiting his reclamation efforts to 30 acres.

Safaa Felefel, another participant in the initial offering, reported investing her entire savings in the land and experiencing substantial losses due to plant mortality, with only olive trees surviving. She lamented, "The current yield from the land is insufficient to cover my installment payments to the NECDC, despite having acquired the land eight years ago."

Expert opinions diverge regarding the farmers' prior awareness of the salinity issue before land acquisition. Ahmed Fawzy Diab contends that water salinity has been a known factor for some time. Prior to the 1.5 million feddan project, individuals, often referred to as squatters, had already reclaimed areas in Al-Maghra. Abbas Sharaqi, a professor of geology and resources at Cairo University, attributes the crisis, in part, to the expedited implementation of the project. He stated, "The project was implemented hastily, lacking adequate prior research. Well drilling occurred without sufficient investigation and at excessively close intervals. Consequently, the implementing agency may not have clearly identified salinity levels. These shortcomings subsequently became evident through declining well efficiency and increased salinity."
