The study determined that a majority (79%) of private wells contain slightly saline water, exhibiting salinity concentrations ranging from 1,552 to 4,385 mg/L. Only 21% of private wells contained relatively fresh water, with lower salinity levels (504–1,496 mg/L).

The situation is more pronounced in government-managed wells, where 80% of the aquifer exhibits saline water conditions, and the remaining 20% displays highly saline water (5011–6693 mg/L). Notably, no fresh water was detected in these wells.

Dr. Saad Muhalal, Professor of Water Sciences at the Desert Research Center and a lead researcher on the study, attributes the elevated salinity levels to the traditional practice of farmers and local residents drilling shallow wells into the Miocene carbonate aquifer. This aquifer, while closest to the surface, is inherently more saline. The relative ease of extraction, requiring only 10 to 20 meters of depth for water flow, has contributed to its widespread use. However, Dr. Muhalal emphasizes that the haphazard drilling practices, lacking proper engineering design, are a primary cause for concern.

He further explains: "This unregulated drilling has led to a critical issue: the mixing of freshwater from the deep Nubian aquifer—a strategic national reserve—with the brackish water from the upper Miocene aquifer. This mixing process is actively degrading the overall water quality due to the proliferation of poorly constructed wells drilled by farmers." Dr. Muhalal characterizes the situation as an utter catastrophe for farmers, stating that the lack of proper well insulation and piping results in the re-introduction of extracted water back into the aquifer, leading to further salinization of both the well and the surrounding land.
