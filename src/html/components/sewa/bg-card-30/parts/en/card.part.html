Shwayrif shares, "Water in Siwa was once allocated on a rotational basis. In the past, we received water consistently. Nowadays, our turn arrives every 21 days, or sometimes just once a month." This traditional method, which depended on the "Hassab"—an individual who precisely calculated dates using the sun, stars, and shadows—ensured a level of fairness for farmers. However, as time went on, the growing demand for water and the expansion of agricultural land within the oasis led to a decline in the system's accuracy. Consequently, numerous complaints about inequitable distribution and inadequate supplies began to surface.

Siwa is home to a unique variety of water sources, ranging from ancient Roman springs to modern wells utilizing deep pumps. However, Shwayrif argues that having an abundance of water does not guarantee equitable access. He points out that some investors, who utilize solar-powered pumps during the summer months, are depleting the water levels in nearby wells, which adversely affects the agricultural lands of local residents like him. While hesitant to frame the issue as a conflict between "locals versus outsiders," Shwayrif has witnessed over the past fifty years the detrimental effects of excessive water extraction on the oasis's delicate balance and its historical agricultural lands.
